## 1. Executive Brief

**The opportunity is real but small at the scale you can execute solo.** Sri Lanka has ~81,000 small-and-medium enterprises with real payroll complexity (11–300 employees), heavily concentrated in the Western Province. No competitor combines self-built automation with SME-affordable managed service — the market splits into cheap self-serve software (Better HR) and expensive white-glove BPO (Infomate, H-Connect, hSenid), with a gap in between. That gap is real, but it's not large enough or defended enough to matter as a moat — any of your competitors could close it in 3–6 months if they wanted to.

**Financially**, a solo/part-time Year 1 realistically nets **LKR 10,000–460,000** (base case ~LKR 460K, bear case near break-even) — not the LKR 780K your notes projected. This is not a business that changes your financial life in year one. It becomes interesting in Year 2–3 once you go full-time and add staff, projecting to roughly LKR 2.9M net (Year 2) and LKR 6.9M net (Year 3) in a base case — but those numbers depend on client acquisition rates that are unproven and currently based on your assumption, not evidence.

**Your real edge isn't the software — it's distribution.** You have a warm network (Hayleys vendors, PPS/PPE manufacturing contacts, IT industry) that competitors don't have easy access to for SME-tier deals. That network gets you to your first 5–8 clients cheaply. After that, growth depends on sales and service execution, which is not where your 22 years of experience gives you an edge.

**Recommendation: Go, but payroll-first, part-time, no upfront spend, and treat Year 1 as a paid market test, not a business launch.** See Section 10 for the specific first step.

## 2. Market Sizing — TAM / SAM / SOM

### Methodology and sources

Sri Lanka's Department of Census and Statistics ran its last full Economic Census in 2013/14, counting **1.017 million SME establishments** in the non-agricultural sector ([DCS](https://www.statistics.gov.lk/Industry/StaticalInformation/SMESurvey2023)). A new e-Economic Census (2025/26) is underway but not yet published, so 2013/14 is the most recent full count available — flagged as **dated data**. MSMEs represent **99% of all business establishments** and **75% of the employed workforce** in Sri Lanka ([ILO](https://www.ilo.org/publications/impact-multiple-crises-sri-lankas-micro-small-and-medium-sized-enterprises)), and contribute **52% of GDP**.

Sri Lanka's official SME size classification ([National SME Policy Framework](http://www.sed.gov.lk/sedweb/en/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/SME-fram-work_eng.pdf)):

| Tier | Employees | Annual turnover |
|---|---|---|
| Micro | 1–10 | < LKR 15M |
| Small | 11–50 | LKR 15M–250M |
| Medium | 51–300 | LKR 250M–750M |

The same 2013/14 census gives the size breakdown: **micro 91.8%, small 7.0%, medium 1.0%, large 0.2%**.

### TAM (Total Addressable Market)

A **micro** enterprise (1–10 people) almost never has payroll complexity worth paying to outsource — the owner runs it themselves, often informally, often cash-in-hand. Your realistic addressable universe is **small + medium** enterprises (11–300 employees), where EPF/ETF/PAYE compliance is unavoidable and manual payroll starts to hurt.

- Total MSMEs (2013/14): 1,017,000
- Small (7.0%): ~71,190
- Medium (1.0%): ~10,170
- **TAM = Small + Medium ≈ 81,360 enterprises**

No published figure exists for what % of these currently outsource HR/payroll — this isn't tracked by DCS or ILO. **[ASSUMPTION]** Based on competitor client-count signals (Section 3) — hSenid serves 650 companies *globally*, Lankastaff and H-Connect appear to serve low hundreds combined domestically — Sri Lanka's formal HR/payroll outsourcing penetration among small+medium enterprises is likely **under 5%**. That's the headroom, but it also means the market hasn't been proven to want this at scale — could be genuine whitespace, or could be a market that doesn't buy.

### SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market)

Western Province generates **42.4% of national GDP** (2024, down from 43.7% in 2023) and **47.6% of industrial output** ([Central Bank of Sri Lanka](https://www.cbsl.gov.lk/en/pgdp-2022) via [EconomyNext](https://economynext.com/sri-lankas-western-province-gdp-share-expands-in-worst-currency-crisis-145059/)). Using GDP share as a density proxy for where small/medium enterprises with money to spend are concentrated:

- Small + Medium in Western Province ≈ 81,360 × 43% ≈ **34,985 enterprises**

That number is still too generous — it includes informal operations that keep payroll in an Excel sheet run by the owner's relative and will never pay a BPO. **[ASSUMPTION]** Applying a 35% "formality filter" (registered, EPF/ETF-compliant, has an actual person doing payroll admin who could be replaced by your service) — a judgment call, not sourced, because no dataset breaks this out:

- **SAM ≈ 12,245 enterprises** in Western Province that are realistic outsourcing candidates.

### SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market — your first 2 years)

This isn't a market-share calculation — it's a capacity calculation. You're solo, part-time, no marketing budget, selling through warm introductions. The constraint isn't demand, it's your own hours.

The financial model in Section 6 works this bottom-up: **base case ~6 clients by end of Year 1, ~19 by end of Year 2.** As a share of the 12,245-enterprise SAM, that's 0.05%–0.16% — i.e., **market size is not your constraint for at least the first 3 years.** Don't spend more research effort refining TAM/SAM further; spend it on sales execution.

### Market trends

- **Post-COVID remote work:** No Sri Lanka-specific data found on this driving HR outsourcing specifically. Global HR tech market context below is the best available proxy.
- **Economic crisis (2022–24), effect on outsourcing demand — mixed, not simply positive:** GDP contracted 7.8% (2022) and 7.9% (H1 2023) ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/business/sri-lankas-economy-shrinks-78-in-2022-1.94485001)). 89% of surveyed MSMEs were severely/very severely affected, and 20.2% of MSMEs that existed in 2018 had closed by end of 2022 ([DCS/ILO survey](https://www.industry.gov.lk/web/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/MSME-Presentation-English.pdf)). This cuts both ways: surviving SMEs have less budget for anything non-essential (bad for you), but same SMEs are also under more pressure to cut fixed headcount costs and outsource what they can (good for you). Separately, **535,000+ Sri Lankans emigrated for foreign employment between Jan 2022–Sep 2023** ([ILO](https://www.ilo.org/publications/sri-lanka%E2%80%99s-labour-market-during-economic-crisis-2022-2023)), tightening the local white-collar labour market — this makes it harder and more expensive for an SME to hire and retain an in-house HR/payroll person, which is a genuine tailwind for outsourcing. **Net read: crisis is a moderate tailwind for outsourcing demand, but a headwind on SME ability to pay for it — don't assume it's unambiguously positive.**
- **Government digital transformation:** ICTA + Ministry of Industry & Entrepreneurship Development are rolling out an SME digitalization support program, first phase across 17 districts in 2025, remaining 8 by mid-2026 ([ICTA](https://www.icta.lk/projects/sri-lanka-go-digital-program-empowering-entrepreneurs-with-applied-knowledge-on-digitizing-the-sme-sector/)). This is a training/consultancy program, not a cash grant you or your clients can draw on directly — worth watching as a channel (partner with it for lead generation) but not a funding source.
- **Global HR tech/BPO market:** Estimates vary by methodology — HR BPO specifically: **$60.2B (2024) → $80B (2030)** at 4.9% CAGR, or a broader HR outsourcing/consultancy definition at **$58.7B (2025) → $88.3B (2030)** at 8.4% CAGR ([Outsource Accelerator / Research and Markets](https://news.outsourceaccelerator.com/hr-bpo-market-to-hit-80bn/)). Consistent theme across sources: mid-to-high single-digit growth, driven by compliance complexity, AI-enabled HR analytics, and remote/distributed workforce management. This is a macro tailwind, not something you can extract local value from directly — Sri Lanka is a rounding error in this global figure.

### Sector breakdown — which segment is most underserved and profitable

| Sector | Your access | Underserved? | Payroll complexity | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Manufacturing SMEs (PPS/PPE network)** | Strong (warm intros) | Moderate — likely already served by generalist providers | High (shift workers, overtime, EPF/ETF-heavy) | **Best beachhead** — access beats everything else at your stage |
| **IT/tech startups** | Strong (you speak the language) | Low — many already on Zoho People/self-serve tools, tech-savvy enough to DIY | Low-medium | Good for recruitment/onboarding add-on, weak for payroll-first |
| **Retail & hospitality** | Weak (no existing network) | High — genuinely underserved, thin margins, high staff turnover | Medium-high (shift-based, hourly) | Attractive later, not a Year 1 fit — cold outreach required |
| **Professional services (law, accounting)** | Weak-moderate | Moderate — small headcount, but compliance-anxious | Low headcount, high compliance sensitivity | Good compliance-audit upsell target, not a volume play |

**Verdict:** manufacturing is the correct beachhead — not because it's the most "underserved" in the abstract, but because it's the only segment where your network removes the hardest part of B2B sales (getting the first meeting). Retail/hospitality is the theoretically larger underserved opportunity but requires cold outreach you can't sustain part-time.

### Indirect threats

- **Will Zoho People / greytHR kill the SME BPO market?** No — they compete with Better HR (self-serve software), not with a managed service. Zoho People even has a **native greytHR payroll integration** for the Sri Lanka/India corridor ([Zoho Help](https://help.zoho.com/portal/en/kb/people/administrator-guide/payroll-integrations/articles/greythr-payroll-zohopeople)), meaning SMEs can self-assemble a "good enough" stack cheaply. The real threat isn't that SaaS kills BPO — it's that SaaS raises the bar for what "good enough DIY" looks like, shrinking the pool of SMEs willing to pay a premium for a human to do it for them.
- **Could a fintech (PayHere, DirectPay) add payroll and eat this space?** Weak evidence either way — both are payment gateways (checkout/subscription/recurring billing APIs), not payroll-disbursement products, and no signal was found of either building toward payroll ([payhere.lk/fees](https://www.payhere.lk/fees/), [directpay.lk](https://www.directpay.lk/)). **[ASSUMPTION]** Low near-term risk — payroll requires compliance domain expertise (EPF/ETF/PAYE calculation logic) that a payment gateway doesn't have and isn't obviously incentivized to build for a market this small.
- **What's stopping a large Indian BPO from entering Sri Lanka?** Nothing structural — but nothing pulling them in either. India's own SME HR-tech market is large enough (RazorpayX Payroll: 10,000+ companies; Keka: enterprise clients including Honda, Godrej) that a Sri Lankan market of ~12,000 realistic SAM accounts isn't worth the market-entry cost for an Indian player already winning at home. **Real barrier protecting you here is market size, not your capability.**

### Your moat — an honest assessment

- **What you actually have:** zero development cost (you build it yourself), a warm network that gets you past the hardest part of B2B sales (the first meeting), and enough runway (day job) to survive a slow start that would kill a funded competitor's unit economics.
- **What you don't have:** a technology moat. Payroll automation for Sri Lanka's EPF/ETF/PAYE rules is a solved problem — Better HR, hSenid, and multiple others have already built it. **[ASSUMPTION]** A competent developer could replicate your MVP payroll engine in **4–8 weeks**, not months. Your tech is a **table-stakes requirement to compete, not a differentiator.**
- **Real barrier to entry for others wanting to do what you're doing:** low. This is a business where the barrier is patient, unglamorous sales execution over 18–24 months — not technology, not capital. That cuts both ways: it's why you can enter, and it's why anyone else can too, including a Better HR or hSenid product manager who decides to build an SME managed-service tier.
- **How you actually defend this:** not through the software. Through switching costs (once a client's payroll history and compliance records live in your system, moving is painful) and through being embedded in networks (Hayleys vendors, PPS/PPE) that a new entrant would have to build from scratch.

## 4. Technology Strategy

### Build vs. buy vs. partner

| Component | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Payroll engine** | Build (Python) | Your stated zero-cost advantage. EPF/ETF/PAYE logic is well-documented public information — no reason to buy. |
| **HRIS/employee portal** | Build (React/Node) | Same logic — this is CRUD + calculation, squarely inside 22 years of engineering experience. |
| **Compliance updates (rule changes)** | **Partner** — retain a labour lawyer or use EFC's HR & Legal Solutions unit for review | This is the one place a coding error becomes a client's legal liability (surcharges up to 50% of overdue EPF/ETF, see Section 7). Don't self-certify compliance logic without a second set of eyes. |
| **Payment collection** | **Buy** — PayHere or DirectPay for client invoicing/subscription billing | Undifferentiated infrastructure, not worth building. |
| **Accounting integration** | Partner/buy — integrate with whatever accounting tool your clients already use (likely QuickBooks or a local equivalent) rather than building your own | You don't want to become an accounting software vendor by accident. |

### Automation opportunities

- **100% automatable today:** payroll calculation (gross-to-net, EPF/ETF/PAYE), payslip generation and distribution, leave balance tracking, attendance-to-payroll reconciliation, statutory report generation (EPF C-forms etc.).
- **Where AI/LLM genuinely fits:** CV screening and shortlisting (highest-value use — this is the most time-consuming manual task in recruitment), first-pass compliance-document checking (flagging missing fields/inconsistencies, not final sign-off), plain-language payslip/policy explanations for employees (reduces support-ticket volume). **[ASSUMPTION]** Do not use LLMs for final compliance determinations — liability sits with you, not the model.
- **How the moat gets stronger with each client:** not through better code — through **data**. Each client's payroll history, compliance record, and edge cases (unusual allowances, past EPF corrections) becomes harder to migrate away from over time. Design the product so historical data has switching-cost value, not just current-month processing value.

### First 10 clients — the actual playbook

1. **List every PPS/PPE and Hayleys vendor contact with 11–300 employees.** Not a vague "network more" — a literal spreadsheet, ranked by relationship warmth.
2. **Offer the first 1–2 clients a free 60-day payroll pilot**, explicitly framed as "help me stress-test this before I charge for it" — removes their risk, gets you real production data and a case study.
3. **Convert pilot clients to paying (LKR 750/employee/month baseline) at day 60**, and ask each one for **2 named introductions** to other SMEs in their sector — this is your actual growth engine, not ads.
4. **Publish one case study per converted client** (with permission) on LinkedIn — this is your only marketing content cost, and it's free.
5. **Target sequence:** manufacturing (via PPS/PPE) for clients 1–5, then use those manufacturing case studies to open doors with adjacent manufacturing SMEs outside your direct network for clients 6–10.
6. **Do not chase IT startups or retail/hospitality for the first 10** — wrong beachhead per Section 2, dilutes your limited part-time hours.

## 6. Financial Model

### Revenue model comparison

| Model | How it works | Fits SME budget? | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Per-employee-per-month (PEPM)** | e.g. LKR 750/employee | Yes — scales naturally with client size, easy to justify | **Primary model** — predictable, recurring, matches how Better HR and the market already price |
| **Flat monthly retainer** | e.g. LKR 50,000 flat | Only fair for one client size — overcharges small clients, undercharges large ones | Use only for the **full HR retainer** tier (Year 3+), not payroll |
| **Per-transaction** | Per payslip, per hire | Fair, but revenue is unpredictable month to month — harder to plan around | Use for **recruitment** (per-placement fee) only |
| **Tiered packages** | Basic/Pro/Enterprise | Gives an upsell path | Introduce in Year 2 once you have >10 clients to justify the packaging complexity |

**Verdict: PEPM for payroll (recurring core), per-transaction for recruitment, flat retainer only for the Year 3+ full-suite tier.** This matches what maximizes LTV (recurring PEPM compounds) while staying legible and affordable to a first-time SME buyer.

### Unit economics

*(Full calculation in the model below; all figures rounded)*

- **Avg client size:** 20 employees **[ASSUMPTION — blended estimate across small/medium tiers]**
- **PEPM rate:** LKR 750/employee/month — positioned above Better HR's LKR 399 self-serve software (you're selling a managed service, not just a tool) and below unpublished-but-implied Infomate/H-Connect enterprise BPO rates
- **Avg revenue per client/month:** LKR 15,000
- **Gross margin (payroll line):** ~75% **[ASSUMPTION]** — high because the software is self-built (no COGS) and delivery is your own time, not paid staff, in Year 1
- **Assumed retention:** 30 months **[ASSUMPTION — no Sri Lanka-specific HR-outsourcing retention data exists]** — payroll has moderate-high switching costs once integrated
- **LTV (margin-adjusted):** ≈ LKR 337,500
- **CAC — referral/warm network:** ≈ LKR 5,000 (notional — your time cost) → **LTV:CAC ≈ 67:1**
- **CAC — paid LinkedIn (Year 2+ scenario):** ≈ LKR 90,000 → **LTV:CAC ≈ 3.8:1** — still healthy, but a different business than the referral-driven Year 1

**The entire economics of Year 1 depend on referral CAC staying near zero. If you have to pay to acquire clients from month one, the model gets materially worse.**

## 8. Strategic Questions — Direct Answers

1. **Should I even do this?** Yes, but as a bounded, part-time experiment — not because the market is huge (it isn't, relative to your effort — see Section 2 SOM), but because the downside is genuinely small (near-zero-cost tech, near break-even bear case) and the upside (Year 3 ~LKR 6.9M net with a real team) is worth testing for.
2. **Sole proprietorship or PVT Ltd?** **Sole proprietorship for Year 1.** Lower setup cost and complexity while you're testing demand. PVT Ltd tax rate is lower (**12% up to LKR 5M net profit** vs. sole trader **16% above LKR 2M** — [source](https://simplebooks.com/srilanka/corporation-better-than-sole-trader/)) and offers liability protection, but that advantage only matters once profit and client-facing credibility require it — convert once you're hiring (Year 2–3), not before.
3. **Payroll-first or full-suite?** **Payroll-first.** It's the recurring-revenue anchor, the most automatable line, and the one where "we do this so you don't have to" is the easiest sell. Recruitment and compliance are natural upsells once trust is established — leading with them first means a harder, less-automatable sale.
4. **B2B or B2C-adjacent (gig workers)?** **Pure B2B SME.** Gig-economy worker services (PickMe drivers, freelancers) is a structurally different business — different buyer, different unit economics, different compliance regime (they're often not EPF/ETF-covered employees at all). No research supports this being a natural extension; treat it as a separate, later decision if at all.
5. **Geographic expansion timeline?** Western Province only through Year 1–2 (matches your SAM and your network). Consider rest-of-island expansion once you have a repeatable, non-founder-dependent sales process (Year 3+). Regional expansion (Maldives, Bangladesh) is not supported by any research here — no evidence was found suggesting Sri Lanka-specific payroll/compliance expertise transfers to those markets, and it should be treated as out of scope until Sri Lanka itself is proven at scale.
6. **Exit strategy?** Realistically, this is a **lifestyle-to-small-business trajectory**, not a venture outcome. IPO is not credible at this market size (compare: hSenid, a real public HR tech company, took decades to reach LKR 1.6B revenue). Acquisition by a player like Infomate is conceivable *if* you build genuine SME-tier distribution they don't have — but that's a Year 3+ conversation, not a plan to build toward now.

None of these are "solo founder, part-time, bootstrapped" stories at the scale they eventually reached — they're evidence for the ceiling, not evidence that your specific approach will get there. Your Year 1–2 plan should be read against Sprout's and Workpay's *early* years, not their current scale.

## 10. Go/No-Go Recommendation

### **GO — but scoped as a bounded market test, not a business launch.**

The market is real (81,360 TAM, ~12,245 realistic SAM in Western Province), underserved in the specific gap between self-serve software and enterprise BPO, and your downside is genuinely small: near-zero build cost, break-even bear case, no debt, no external capital at risk. The upside case (Year 3 ~LKR 6.9M net, 39 clients, real team) is worth finding out if it's achievable — but nothing in this research suggests it's *likely* without disciplined, patient sales execution that has nothing to do with your engineering strength.

**What would make this a No:** if your bear case (near-zero Year 1 net, 3 clients) isn't an acceptable outcome for the time you'd spend getting there — because that's the realistic downside, not the tail risk.

### The first concrete step

**Do not register a company or build the full platform yet.** Build the smallest possible version of the payroll calculator (gross-to-net + EPF/ETF/PAYE for one client's actual payroll structure), and run it as a free 60-day pilot for **one** PPS/PPE-network contact, in parallel with your day job, before spending a single rupee or hour on anything else in this document.

That single pilot answers the two questions that actually determine whether the rest of this plan is worth pursuing: **(1) does a real SME's payroll data break your calculation logic in ways the research couldn't predict, and (2) will a warm contact actually convert to a referral after the free period, or does the model's 67:1 LTV:CAC assumption collapse on contact with reality.** Everything else in this document — pricing, GTM sequencing, PVT Ltd conversion, hiring — is downstream of what that one pilot tells you.
